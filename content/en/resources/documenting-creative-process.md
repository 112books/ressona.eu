The creative process disappears. The piece remains; the rehearsal, the draft, the conversation that changed the project's direction — all of that evaporates unless someone records it. And it's a double loss: loss for the artist's own memory, and loss of concrete opportunities (grants, residencies, social media content, application dossiers).

The good news is that documenting the process requires neither much time nor professional equipment. What it requires, above all, is a simple system and the habit of following it.

## What documenting the process is actually for

Four real, concrete uses:

1. **Grants and funding**: many calls for applications ask you to document the research process, not just the result. A dossier with rehearsal photos, working notes and process materials is far more convincing than a report written after the fact. When you have accumulated material, the application takes hours, not days.

2. **Artist residencies**: residencies select projects in process, not finished projects. Documentation material is the proof that the process is real — that there is active research underway, not an abstract idea.

3. **Social media**: behind-the-scenes content is one of the highest-engagement formats for artist accounts. Not because audiences want to see everything — but because the humanity of the process connects in a way that a finished result often doesn't. An imperfect rehearsal clip can travel further than a polished production photo.

4. **Your own memory**: five years from now, when someone asks how you made that piece, you'll want something more than recollection. Creative processes contain decisions, mistakes and discoveries that disappear if they aren't recorded.

## What to document

You don't need to document everything. A chaotic archive with hundreds of unsorted photos is almost as useless as having nothing. A few well-selected, well-organised items work far better:

- **Photos of your workspace and materials**: the table, the rehearsal stage, elements under construction. Easy to take with a phone, valuable for dossiers and social media. They don't need to be good photos — authenticity matters more than composition.

- **Process notes**: they don't need to be good either. A text note on your phone recording what happened today in the studio, rehearsal or writing session. Regularity matters more than writing quality. Three sentences at the end of each session is enough.

- **Short videos**: 30 seconds of rehearsal, a musical phrase being constructed, a movement being defined. No editing required. They work as memory and as raw material for social media.

- **Sketches and working materials**: annotated scores, scripts in progress, design sketches, set plans. Photograph them and store them. Physical documents age and get lost; a photo in the cloud doesn't.

## How to do it without interrupting your work

The main problem with documentation is that it can interrupt creative flow. The solution is not to document less, but to separate the moment of creating from the moment of documenting.

Don't document while you're in deep work. Instead, set a fixed moment at the end of each session — the last five minutes of rehearsal, just before closing the studio, once a week on Friday — to take a photo, leave a voice note, or save the day's materials.

Three minimal habits that work:

1. **One photo at the end of each working session**: what you made, the space, a material detail. It doesn't need to be artistic — it needs to be informative.

2. **A three-sentence text note**: what was worked on, what worked, what's still pending. Can be left as a voice note if writing feels disruptive.

3. **A 30-second video once a week**: a fragment of what you're making, unedited, as a time capsule of the process at that moment.

With these three habits, in one month you have more than enough material for a residency application and content for six weeks of social media.

## Where to store it

Organisation is as important as capture. A simple, consistent folder structure across projects prevents the chaotic archive:

```
/Projects/
  /Project-A-2026/
    /photos/
    /videos/
    /notes/
    /materials/
```

For automatic syncing from your phone, [Google Drive](https://drive.google.com/) or [iCloud](https://www.icloud.com/) handle it without configuration. The key is that the folder on your phone syncs directly to the project folder, with no intermediate steps.

## How to use the accumulated material

Documented material doesn't use itself. A minimal habit of extracting value from it is needed:

- **For social media**: once a month, review the month's material and select 4–6 items to publish. They don't need to be perfect — imperfection is part of the authenticity of the process. A 30-second reel of rehearsal footage can have more impact than a produced photo shoot.

- **For dossiers and applications**: when a call for applications arrives, the material already exists. The work is selecting and ordering it, not creating from scratch. This reduces application preparation from days to hours.

- **For the project archive**: once a year, or when each project ends, create an archive with the best moments of the process. Ten years from now, when someone asks you to talk about that project or when you want to revisit it, you'll have a complete record.

A note on professional photography: phone photos of process work perfectly for social media and application dossiers. ## Process material as identity

There is an additional benefit that goes beyond applications and social media: documenting the process builds the artist's narrative. Who you are as a creator, how you work, what decisions you make — all of this lives in the process material. When it comes time to explain yourself to a programmer, a journalist or a collaborator, you'll have more than words.
