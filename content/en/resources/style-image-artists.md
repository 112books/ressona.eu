There's a photograph of David Bowie from 1973, at the Hammersmith Odeon, the last night of the Ziggy Stardust tour. He's wearing a silk shirt in vivid colours, light skirt-like trousers, orange hair, eyes painted with deliberate asymmetry. It's not make-up. It's not a costume. It's the character — and the character is the work.

Most artists who work on their image don't do it because they want to «sell themselves». They do it because they've understood something that takes years to put into words: **appearance is part of the artistic language**, not an external wrapping placed on top.

## Image as extension of the work

Patti Smith doesn't wear that white shirt and black trousers by accident. Rosalía doesn't choose her contemporary flamenco dresses to follow a trend. Nick Cave doesn't dress in black because it matches his shoes. Each of them has built a **personal visual palette** that is inseparable from what they do, how they do it and for whom they do it.

Caetano Veloso in the 1970s broke with the aesthetic of the MPB of his era by adopting an image that blended Brazilian tradition with Anglo-Saxon psychedelic pop. His clothes were political. His image was a statement of intent.

When we talk about artistic image, we're not talking about «looking good». We're talking about **coherence between your art and your presence in the world**.

A jazz pianist who plays Monk but presents themselves with a 1990s rock aesthetic isn't necessarily «wrong» — they're just sending two contradictory messages at once. The audience doesn't know where to look.

## Personal visual palette: what it is and how to build it

Your personal visual palette isn't the clothes you wear on stage. It's the system of visual elements that represent you consistently across all contexts: in front of your audience, in press photos, on social media, on your website.

It includes:

- The colours that appear recurrently in your image (clothes, design, environments)
- The textures and materials that identify you (silk, linen, leather, natural, industrial)
- The relationship between your body and space (architectural, minimal, exuberant, chaotic)
- The degree of formality or rupture with context (classic in a punk venue; casual in a high-culture space)
- The photographic style that accompanies you: hard or soft light, colour or black and white, close-up or distance

You don't need to have everything defined from day one. But you do need to build it consciously, not by inertia.

## Coherence between the physical and the digital

This is where many artists lose the thread. They build one image on stage and a completely different one on Instagram. They wear carefully considered costumes at concerts but post everyday photos with no connection to their project's aesthetic. The result is that new audiences who discover them online have no reason to connect them with the artist they saw at the festival.

Digital identity must be a projection of artistic identity, not a separate channel. This doesn't mean everything you post on Instagram needs to look like a fashion shoot. It means there must be a recognisable **connecting thread**: the colours, the light, the type of shots, the tone. The people who follow you should feel that everything belongs to the same universe.

## The danger of following trends

There's a trap many emerging artists fall into: building their image based on what they see working for others. The problem is that by the time they're implementing it, the trend has already moved on — and on top of that, it isn't genuine.

The artistic image that lasts is the one that comes from within. It doesn't mean you can't draw inspiration from references — it means you filter them through your own judgement, your own context, your own voice. Björk copies no one. Patti Smith either. And that's precisely why we're still talking about them decades later.

Visual authenticity isn't a trend. It's what remains when trends pass.

## Where to start if you're at the beginning

You don't need a stylist, photographer and designer from day one. But you can do some things right now:

Look at your last ten public photos as if you were someone seeing you for the first time. What do they say about you? What world do they evoke? Does it match what you do artistically?

Find three or four visual references — artists you admire not for the music or the work, but for how they present themselves to the world. Analyse what they do: the colours, the light, the consistency between stage and digital.

Define three words that describe the aesthetic of your project. Rigorous, warm, wild, minimalist, exuberant... When you choose clothes or select a photo, return to these words.
