A good photo session is an investment. To get the most out of it, preparation is just as important as the photographer's talent. Here's everything you need to know.

## Before the session

The work starts long before you arrive at the studio or location.

<div class="resource-steps">

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">01</span>

**Define the goal.** What will the material be used for? Press kit for festivals? Social media profile? Dossier for theatres? The answer determines formats, lighting, clothing and expressions. A photo for a concert poster is not the same as a LinkedIn profile shot.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">02</span>

**Collect references.** Find photos you like — of musicians, actors, artists in your field. Not to copy them, but to communicate the tone you want to the photographer: formal or relaxed, intimate or spectacular, colour or black and white. A Pinterest board or a Google Photos album works perfectly.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**Choose your outfits in advance.** Bring at least three options. Avoid very fine patterns (they create moiré on screen), pure whites (they blow out with flash) and brand logos (they date the photos and can create rights issues). Try everything the day before and make sure there are no wrinkles.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**Rest and take care of your appearance.** The night before: enough sleep, lots of water, no alcohol. If you have skin that shows under-eye bags or redness easily, apply something cold in the morning. For actors and visual artists, professional makeup should be arranged in advance — it's not an extra: it's part of the session.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">05</span>

**Talk to the photographer.** A 20-minute call or meeting beforehand makes all the difference. Share your references, explain who you are, what your artistic style is, what kind of spaces you perform in. A good photographer listens and adapts their eye to your identity.
</div>

</div>

<h3>Arrive early</h3>
<p>Twenty minutes early to change, breathe, get used to the space. The first shots always take a bit of warming up — don't rush them.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="resource-checklist__item">
<span class="resource-checklist__icon" aria-hidden="true">

<div>
<h3>Ask to see photos during the session</h3>
<p>You don't have to wait until the end to know whether the light is working or the pose is right. A professional will share images in real time so you can adjust.</p>

<div class="resource-checklist__item">

<h3>Forget about the camera</h3>
<p>The best photos come when you stop posing. Talk to the photographer, laugh at a mistake, move naturally. The real moment is the authentic one.</p>

<h3>Bring your instrument, symbol or object</h3>
<p>For musicians, the instrument is part of the identity. For actors, a prop that represents your style. For visual artists, brushes, a sculpture, work materials. Contextualise who you are.</p>

## How many images do you need?

It's about utility, not quantity:

- **3–5 profile photos** (square and vertical crop, clean or blurred background)
- **2–3 action or contextual photos** (playing, rehearsing, creating)
- **1–2 group photos** if you're part of a company or band
- **Landscape version** for website headers, posters and press

Ask the photographer to deliver high resolution (at least 2400px wide) in JPEG/PNG. If you need RAW files for further retouching, agree on it beforehand.

## Rights and deliverables

Agree in writing (or by email) what uses are included in the price: social media, website, press, posters... If not specified, usage rights may be limited. [Read our article on image rights](/en/resources/image-rights-promotional/) to understand the implications.
