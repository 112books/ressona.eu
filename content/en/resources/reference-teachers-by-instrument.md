Learning an instrument is inseparable from learning who plays it well. Not to imitate, but to calibrate: to understand how far the instrument can go in the hands of someone who truly masters it, what music it has generated, what schools of thought have developed around it. Every instrument has its giants, its founding pedagogues, and its contemporary teachers who have known how to pass on what the greats were doing. Here is a selection by instrument.

## Classical Guitar

- **Andrés Segovia**: the founder of the modern repertoire. His method and editions are still in circulation. Listening to him means understanding where everything comes from.
- **John Williams**: impeccable technique, enormous repertoire. His recordings of Bach and Rodrigo are undisputed references.
- **David Russell**: for those who want to understand musicality above pyrotechnics. Many masterclasses available on YouTube.
- For classical guitar online: **[Bradford Werner's](https://www.thisisclassicalguitar.com/)** channel is one of the most comprehensive free resources on the web.

## Electric Guitar / Jazz / Contemporary

- **Wes Montgomery**: the father of modern jazz guitar. The octave technique, the phrasing — it all comes from him.
- **Pat Metheny**: for those who want to understand how the guitar can be an orchestra. His masterclasses and YouTube interviews are excellent pedagogical material.
- **Tommy Emmanuel**: for acoustic fingerstyle guitar. At **[ArtistWorks](https://artistworks.com/)** he offers an online course with personal feedback.
- **Adam Neely** (bass, but his theory videos apply directly to guitar): **[YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@AdamNeely)** — the best free resource for applied music theory.

## Piano

- **Glenn Gould**: for Bach. His Goldberg Variations (1955 and 1981) and his writings on interpretation define a way of thinking about the piano.
- **Martha Argerich**: for Chopin, Schumann, Ravel. Her example teaches that rhythm is the first and last law.
- **Brad Mehldau**: for contemporary jazz. His "Art of the Trio" recordings and solo work show how to integrate Bach, Radiohead and Coltrane into a single language.
- Pedagogical platforms: **[Tonebase](https://tonebase.co/)** — online platform specifically for classical instruments (piano, cello, violin) with professors from institutions like Juilliard and Curtis.

## Voice

- **[Seth Riggs](https://www.sethriggs.com/)**: creator of the Speech Level Singing method. Teacher of Michael Jackson, Stevie Wonder and Barbara Streisand, among many others.
- **[Ingo Titze](https://www.ncvs.org/)**: leading researcher in voice science (National Center for Voice and Speech). For those who want to understand the physiology.
- **[Justin Stoney — New York Vocal Coaching](https://www.youtube.com/@NewYorkVocalCoaching)**: YouTube channel with thousands of free practical videos. One of the best resources for popular singing.
- For classical singing: the method of the great Italian bel canto pedagogues (Lamperti, García) remains the foundation still taught in conservatories worldwide.

## Bass

- **Jaco Pastorius**: redefined what was possible on the electric bass. The album "Jaco Pastorius" (1976) is mandatory listening.
- **Victor Wooten**: extreme technique and musicality. His book "The Music Lesson" is as good a pedagogue as his performances.
- **[Scott's Bass Lessons](https://www.scottsbasslessons.com/)**: the reference online platform for bass. Excellent library of structured content for all styles and levels.

## Drums

- **Tony Williams**: revolutionary jazz drummer of the 1960s with Miles Davis. His timing and rhythmic creativity are still studied.
- **Steve Gadd**: the definitive session musician. He has played on more landmark recordings than probably any other drummer.
- **[Drumeo](https://www.drumeo.com/)**: the best online platform for drums. Instructors like Mike Johnston and Jared Falk. Content for all levels and styles.

## Violin

- **Jascha Heifetz**: the absolute technical standard of the 20th century. Listening to him and comparing him to any other violinist is understanding what is possible.
- **Hilary Hahn**: for contemporary technique and musicality. Very active on social media, sharing her practice process openly.
- **Nathan Milstein**: for unaccompanied Bach. His Partitas and Sonatas are the reference every violinist should know.
- **[Tonebase](https://tonebase.co/)**: violin courses with professors from Juilliard and Colburn.

## Saxophone

- **John Coltrane**: "A Love Supreme" and "Giant Steps" define two poles of what the saxophone can do in jazz.
- **Charlie Parker**: bebop, harmony, speed — everything passes through Bird.
- The **[Jamey Aebersold](https://www.jazzbooks.com/)** method: the way to study jazz with play-alongs that remains the standard. Hundreds of volumes for all levels and styles.

## How to use this map

Listening to the greats is different from imitating them. What studying their recordings provides is calibration: knowing how the instrument sounds when it reaches its maximum, in order to have a clear internal reference. Superficial imitation blocks; deep listening liberates.

A practical exercise: choose a reference player for your instrument and listen to them for a week without your instrument in hand. Analyse the phrasing, the silences, the dynamics. Then go back to playing. The difference is noticeable.

For most instruments, today's online platforms — Tonebase, Drumeo, Scott's Bass Lessons, ArtistWorks — have made accessible a level of teaching that previously required living in New York or Berlin. This doesn't replace a in-person teacher, but it gives a musician anywhere access to the same content that a Juilliard student uses as a supplement.

What no platform replaces is the relationship with a teacher who knows you: how you learn, where you get stuck, which technical habits you repeat. The platform provides content; the teacher provides diagnosis.
