The word "networking" puts many artists off because it conjures a specific image: a room full of people swapping business cards, faking interest and pitching themselves. Nothing that seems compatible with how an artist thinks about their relationships. But artistic networking is not that. At its best, it is the natural byproduct of doing things and finding yourself alongside people doing similar things.

## The difference between networking and selling yourself

Transactional networking — going to an event specifically to leave with three useful contacts — almost never works, because people notice. There is a quality to interactions driven by extraction that is evident to the person receiving them, even when they cannot articulate it consciously.

The networking that does work is what happens as a side effect of being present in the places where the music or art you care about is being made. A festival programmer remembers you if they have seen you perform three times, not if you handed them your card once. A colleague recommends you if you have crossed paths in a real context, not because you connected on LinkedIn during a blitz of outreach.

## Where real networking happens

Five contexts where genuine artistic professional relationships are built:

**1. Festivals where you are an audience member, not a performer.** Attending festivals that programme what you do, as a spectator. Crossing paths with programmers and colleagues in a context without sales pressure. Being seen without anyone having anything to gain immediately.

**2. Artist residencies.** Shared working space generates relationships of an intensity and durability that no networking event can replicate. Sharing a creative process with other artists over weeks creates a kind of professional trust that is very difficult to achieve any other way. Platforms like **[Res Artis](https://www.resartis.org/)** and **[Artfinca](https://www.artfinca.com/)** aggregate residency opportunities globally.

**3. Courses and masterclasses.** Being seen in a learning context. The colleagues you meet at Siena or Dartington remember each other. Sharing a difficult learning experience creates a different kind of bond than sharing a social event.

**4. Occasional collaborations.** Making music or art together, however small the collaboration, is the best way to know someone professionally. A collaboration reveals how someone works under pressure, how they make decisions, how they handle creative conflict. No networking conversation can reveal the same.

**5. Specific online networks.** Not Instagram in general, but concrete professional spaces: musician Discord servers, contemporary dance forums, Facebook communities for cultural managers. Places where people talk about real problems, not the curated version of their career.

## The card that works today

The physical business card is dead in most artistic contexts. What works is a consistent digital profile: when someone who has just seen you perform searches your name on their phone, they find what they need in under fifteen seconds. A clear website, a coherent Instagram, an accessible EPK.

Networking starts offline and continues online — and if your digital presence is chaotic or non-existent, the thread is lost. The person who saw you perform and was interested will not search for you twice. ## How to maintain relationships without being a burden

Most artistic professional relationships die from neglect, not overreach. A brief email when you are passing through someone's city, sharing one of their performances when it is relevant, a note on an opening — small, genuine actions that keep the thread alive without creating any obligation for the other person.

What does not work: the cold email asking for opportunities from someone who knows nothing about you. Sending an email to a festival programmer who has never heard of you is largely pointless unless you have something very specific and relevant to them. Cold contact works much better when it is not completely cold — when there is some prior point of connection, however small.

## The give-first rule

Most professional relationships that work over the long term begin with someone giving something — information, an opportunity, an introduction — without expecting anything in return immediately. Not because it is a strategy, but because it is how communities function.

Sharing a useful resource you know will interest someone. Making an introduction between two people who would benefit from knowing each other. Mentioning someone's work in a context where it is relevant. Actions that cost little and that create the kind of informal reciprocity that underlies every professional network that actually works.

## On social media as a networking tool

Instagram is not networking: it is broadcast. It serves to exist, to be visible, so that people who look for you find that you are real and that you make things. It is not the place where professional relationships are built.

**[LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/)** can be networking if used for genuine professional connections rather than disguised advertising. What does work on networks: responding with substance to posts by people you admire, starting conversations based on the content they publish — not based on what you want to get from them. The difference is evident to the person receiving the message.

## When networking does not work

If you are building relationships but nothing is happening, it is usually for one of three reasons:

**Insufficient digital presence.** People cannot continue the relationship if they cannot find you. If your website does not exist or is confusing, if your Instagram does not reflect who you are artistically, the contact you made at that festival evaporates.

**Lack of shared context.** You are not in the spaces where the people who can help you are. If your art is not visible in the places where programming decisions are made, it is very difficult to be considered no matter how much relationship-building you do.

**What you offer is not clear enough.** If the people you connect with do not know exactly what you do and what kind of project they might need you for, they cannot recommend you or hire you. Clarity about your artistic proposition is the prerequisite for any networking that goes beyond pleasant personal relations.
