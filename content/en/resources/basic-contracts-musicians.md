Most legal problems musicians face don't come from bad intentions. They come from signing without reading, from verbal agreements that each party remembers differently, or from not knowing there was a clause that changed everything.

This isn't legal advice — for that you need a lawyer specialising in intellectual property and entertainment law. What we offer here is the map: which contracts exist, what each one covers, and which points you should always check before signing.

## The performance contract

This is the document that formalises a live performance: who performs, where, when, how much they're paid, and under what conditions.

**What it must contain as a minimum:**

<div class="resource-steps">

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">01</span>

**Identification of the parties.** Full legal name or company name, tax ID number and contact address of the artist (or their representative) and the promoter or venue. If you're performing through a company or as a self-employed professional, that entity must appear — not your personal name.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">02</span>

**Date, time and location.** Exact address, load-in time, soundcheck time, show start time and estimated duration. If there are two sets or specific scheduling conditions, they must be stated in writing.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**Fee and payment terms.** Total amount, whether or not it includes VAT, when payment is due (deposit plus balance, or all at the end), and the payment method (bank transfer, cash, cheque). A verbal agreement on the fee is nearly impossible to prove if there's a dispute.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**Technical rider attached.** The technical rider is part of the performance contract. If the promoter cannot meet any technical requirement, they must communicate this in advance — not on the day of the show.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">05</span>

**Cancellation clause.** What happens if the promoter cancels? What happens if you cancel? Typically: cancellation by the promoter with less than X days' notice means paying 50–100% of the fee. Defining this avoids conflicts.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">06</span>

**Image rights and recording.** Can the performance be recorded? Can it be published? Where? Who owns the rights? If the promoter wants to record the show to post on social media, the terms must be stated in writing.
</div>

</div>

## The rights assignment contract

When you assign rights to a song, an album or any other work, you're transferring — fully or partially — the ability to exploit it commercially. Signing this badly can have consequences years — or decades — later.

**Critical points to check:**

<div>
<h3>Exclusivity and territory</h3>
<p>Is the assignment exclusive (only that company can exploit it) or non-exclusive (you can assign the same rights to others)? Does it cover the whole world or a specific territory? A worldwide exclusive assignment is very different from a non-exclusive assignment for Spain.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="resource-checklist__item">
<span class="resource-checklist__icon" aria-hidden="true">
<svg width="28" height="28" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round"><polyline points="20 6 9 17 4 12"/></svg>
</span>
<div>
<h3>Duration</h3>
<p>Are you assigning the rights for a fixed period (5 years, 10 years) or indefinitely? If there's no expiry date, what conditions allow the assignment to revert? Contracts "for the full term of legal protection of the work" mean 70 years after your death.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h3>Which rights exactly</h3>
<p>Reproduction, distribution, public communication, adaptation, synchronisation (use in audiovisual works)... Each right is independent. A contract may assign distribution rights while retaining synchronisation — and that's where the big money is, in placement for film, advertising or video games.</p>

<h3>Remuneration and royalty statements</h3>
<p>How is the assignment remunerated? One-off payment, percentage royalties, advance against royalties? If there's an advance, what income counts towards recouping it? How are royalty statements issued: annually, bi-annually? Do you have audit rights?</p>

## Contracts with record labels and distributors

The streaming era has changed many standard agreements, but some classic terms remain:

**Record deal:** The label finances production and retains the masters (the original recordings). You, as the artist, receive royalties — typically 15–20% of net income — until the advance is recouped. The masters belong to the label for the duration of the contract, and often indefinitely unless negotiated otherwise.

**Licence contract:** You produce the recording and licence the master for a specific territory and period. You retain ownership of the master. Better for artists who already self-produce.

**Digital distributor:** You don't assign rights — the distributor acts as an intermediary to place your music on streaming platforms. They charge a percentage or annual fee. Read the conditions for reverting masters if you leave the distributor: some retain catalogue for months.

**360° deal:** The label participates in all of the artist's income: live performances, merchandise, advertising, licences. In return, there's usually a commitment to marketing support. Negotiate carefully what percentage applies to each income category.

## A note on SGAE and CEDRO

When you register with SGAE (Spain's performing rights organisation) as an author or publisher, you assign collective management of the public communication and mechanical reproduction rights to your works. SGAE collects and distributes on your behalf. CEDRO (Spain's reproduction rights organisation) performs a similar function for written works and print-related uses.

Things many authors don't know:
- SGAE manages public communication rights (concerts, radio, TV, public spaces) and mechanical reproduction rights (streaming, digital sales). But it does not manage synchronisation rights — those are negotiated directly by the rights holder.
- Registering a work with SGAE is not assigning ownership of it. You remain the author. SGAE manages collection on your behalf in exchange for a commission.
- If you publish music through a digital distributor and are not registered with a collective management organisation, your public communication rights in public spaces are probably not being collected.

## Practical recommendation

For standard performance contracts with moderate fees, a well-drafted template signed by both parties is sufficient. For agreements with labels, platforms, or significant rights assignments, investing in a consultation with a lawyer specialising in intellectual property is one of the best investments you can make as a professional musician.
