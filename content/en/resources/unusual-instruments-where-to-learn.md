Studying an unusual instrument means going against the grain of the system: most schools don't teach it, specialised teachers are few, and pedagogical material is scarce. But the community that forms around a rare instrument tends to be intense, generous and very well connected. Here is a map of resources for some of the less obvious instruments.

## Viola da Gamba

The bowed string instrument of the Baroque era, predecessor of the cello. It has six strings, frets, and a sound unlike any modern instrument.

- **Where to learn**: specialist conservatories in Europe — the **[Schola Cantorum Basiliensis](https://www.schola-cantorum.ch/)** in Basel is one of the leading institutions for early music performance. In the UK, the Royal College of Music and the Royal Academy of Music both offer viola da gamba.
- **Online**: structured courses are scarce. The **[Viola da Gamba Society](https://vdgs.org.uk/)** (UK) has resources and a teacher directory.
- **Reference**: **[Jordi Savall](https://jordisavall.com/)** (Catalan) is the most important viola da gambist in the world. His recordings with Hespèrion XXI are the essential starting point. Paolo Pandolfo is another key contemporary reference.

## Harpsichord

The keyboard instrument that preceded the piano (plucked strings rather than struck). Enormous repertoire: Bach, Scarlatti, Couperin.

- **Where to learn**: major conservatories with early music departments — Basel, The Hague (Royal Conservatoire), London. Summer courses in Italy (Accademia Chigiana, Siena) and Germany are well established.
- **Online**: **[Tonebase](https://tonebase.co/)** has expanded its catalogue to include period instruments, with professors specialising in early repertoire.
- **References**: **[Scott Ross](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCqUJc0ktbdMaCh-wVMVXORw)**, **[Gustav Leonhardt](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCl9ylVFNB29fiiNcCTua6NA)**, **[Trevor Pinnock](https://trevorpinnock.com/)** — three very different interpretive schools representing distinct national traditions and aesthetics.

## Theremin

The only instrument played without physical contact. Controlled by the position of the hands in an electromagnetic field: one vertical antenna for melody, one horizontal for volume.

- **Where to learn**: in-person options are very limited worldwide. The key reference is **[Carolina Eyck](https://carolinaeyck.com/)**, Dutch thereminist who created the main pedagogical method (the "Eyck Method") and offers online courses on her website.
- **Community**: **[Theremin World](https://thereminworld.com/)** — forum, resources and teacher directory. The global community is active and very welcoming for beginners.
- **Where to start**: the **[Moog Theremini](https://www.moogmusic.com/products/theremini)** is the most affordable digital version for learning, with assisted tuning that makes the first steps easier.

## Oud

The plucked string instrument of Arab and Mediterranean origin, predecessor of the European lute and guitar. Central in classical Arab, Turkish and Persian music, and in some flamenco repertoires.

- **Where to learn**: **[Lessonface](https://www.lessonface.com/)** has teachers covering Arab, Turkish and Persian oud traditions, accessible to students anywhere. In London, the **[School of Oriental and African Studies (SOAS)](https://www.soas.ac.uk/)** offers programmes in world music including oud.
- **Online**: **[Arab Instruments](https://www.youtube.com/@ArabInstruments)** on YouTube has useful introductory material for beginners. Lessonface remains the best option for structured one-to-one lessons.
- **Festivals**: world music festivals and ethnomusicology conferences often bring master oud players for workshops — worth checking local programme listings.

## Carillons and Church Bells

The carillon is a percussion instrument of fixed bells, played from a manual keyboard (with the fists, not the fingers) and a pedalboard. There are hundreds of carillons across Europe, many accessible to the public through open concerts.

- **Where to learn**: the world reference school is the **[Royal Carillon School "Jef Denyn" in Mechelen](https://www.beiaardschool.be/)** (Belgium), with summer courses and full professional carillonneur training. Instruction is available in English.
- **Network**: the **[World Carillon Federation](https://www.worldcarillonfederation.org/)** connects schools and performers worldwide and maintains a directory of teachers and instruments.
- **Getting started**: many carillonneurs are open to showing their instrument to curious visitors — contacting your nearest carillon tower is often the best first step.

## Reed Instruments — the Arundo Donax Connection

Arundo donax (giant Mediterranean cane) is the material from which clarinet, oboe, bassoon and saxophone reeds are made. A lively community of luthiers, performers and researchers has formed around this material.

- **[Festival Arundo Donax](https://arundodonax2009.blogspot.com)**: a festival dedicated to reed instruments, with construction workshops, concerts and research into the acoustic properties of cane. Highly specific but extraordinarily rich for oboists, clarinettists and instrument makers.
- **Mediterranean cane traditions**: flaviol, gralla, tenora — instruments central to Catalan popular music culture. These traditions are preserved through direct transmission in folk music associations and popular culture groups.
- For professional oboe and bassoon: making and adjusting your own reeds is a skill that leading performers master. Some chamber music festivals and orchestras offer dedicated reed-making workshops.

## Sitar and Instruments of the Indian Subcontinent

- **Where to learn**: very few schools in most Western cities. Individual teachers, yoga centres and cultural institutes sometimes offer introduction.
- **Online**: **[Sitar Academy Online](https://sitaracademy.com/)** — structured courses in English with teachers in the tradition. The recordings of **Nikhil Banerjee** and **Ravi Shankar** are the unavoidable references.
- **Important note**: classical Indian instruments (sitar, tabla, sarod) traditionally require years of study and a guru-student relationship with a master. Online learning is a useful initial supplement, not a substitute for that relationship.

## Finding Teachers for Any Instrument

When there is no school: **[Lessonface](https://www.lessonface.com/)**, **[TakeLessons](https://takelessons.com/)** and the instrument's own network (societies, federations, specialist festivals). The community around a rare instrument is small but very accessible — an email to the relevant society almost always gets a response. Teachers of unusual instruments tend to be receptive to new learners because the continuity of the tradition matters to them.
