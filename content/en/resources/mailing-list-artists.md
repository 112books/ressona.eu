Social media platforms change their algorithms, limit your organic reach or disappear altogether. Your mailing list doesn't: it's yours, it's direct and it reaches people who have said they want to hear from you. A newsletter has an open rate of 20–40%; an Instagram post reaches 2–5% of your followers organically.

But there is one fundamental condition: the list must be built with explicit consent. Adding addresses without permission isn't a shortcut — it's a legal infringement and a source of serious reputational risk.

## The legal framework: GDPR in practical terms

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) governs how you can collect, store and use email addresses in the European Union. For an artist who wants to send newsletters, the basic rules are:

**Explicit, active consent.** No one can be on your list without having actively opted in. A pre-ticked checkbox is not valid consent. A form where someone types their email and clicks "subscribe" is. Someone following your Instagram account is not consent to receive emails.

**Easy, immediate unsubscribe.** Every email you send must contain a visible, working unsubscribe link. If someone requests removal and you take more than 10 days to act on it, that's an infringement.

**Right of access and erasure.** Any subscriber has the right to ask what data you hold about them and to request its deletion. You must be able to respond and act.

**Consent records.** You must be able to demonstrate how and when each person consented to receive your emails. Modern newsletter tools store this automatically. If you manage lists manually, you need to document it yourself.

**You are the data controller.** If you're self-employed and manage your own list, you are the data controller. Your privacy policy must mention how you process subscriber data.

## Own contacts vs. public contacts

This is the distinction that causes the most errors.

**Own contacts with consent** (can be added):
- People who filled in a subscription form on your website
- People who gave you their business card and explicitly agreed to receive information from you
- People who emailed you asking for information, and whom you've informed you'll add to the list
- Active professional contacts, provided they're informed of how their data is used

**Public contacts** (cannot be added without consent):
- Emails found on websites, directories or social media profiles
- Addresses collected from show programmes, festivals or someone else's press lists
- Instagram, Facebook or TikTok followers — following a profile is not consent to receive emails
- Colleagues or acquaintances who haven't asked to be on your list

The practical rule: if you haven't seen the person click a subscribe button or explicitly agree, you cannot add them.

## How to build your list legally

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">01</span>

**Subscription form on your website.** The foundation of any healthy list. A name and email field, a button, and a clear description of what they'll receive ("Monthly updates: gigs, premieres and exclusive content"). Best with **double opt-in**: the person receives a confirmation email and clicks to confirm. This reduces fake addresses and is more legally robust.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">02</span>

**Privacy policy mention.** The form must include a link to your privacy policy and a line like: "By subscribing, you agree to us processing your data to send you information about [artist name]." It doesn't need to be long: it needs to be clear.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**An incentive to subscribe.** The form alone doesn't grow the list quickly. An incentive helps: early access to tickets, exclusive content (PDF, audio, video), a discount in your shop. Whatever is genuinely relevant to your audience.
</div>

<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**Call to action at gigs and shows.** "If you'd like to know when I'm next performing, leave your email here" — a QR code on the programme, a tablet at the door, or an announcement from the stage. Live audiences are the best source of subscribers.

**Link to the newsletter from social media.** Bio link, pinned post, regular stories. Not to replace social media, but to convert followers into subscribers. A follower belongs to Instagram; a subscriber belongs to you.

## Segmentation: not everyone wants the same thing

A homogeneous list is less effective than a segmented one. The people who follow you don't all want the same information:

- **Fans and general audience**: concert and premiere news, updates. Monthly or bi-monthly frequency.
- **Press and journalists**: press releases, media materials, premieres. Occasional and relevant, not regular.
- **Programmers and venues**: project presentation, availability, technical rider. Very selective — once per season at most.
- **Professional peers**: collaborations, open calls, sector news. Could be a separate segment or list entirely.

Most newsletter tools allow you to tag subscribers by segment and send targeted campaigns to each group. It's not complex to manage, but it requires thinking about it from the start.

## Website integration

The subscription form should be in visible places:

- **Header or hero**: maximum visibility, maximum conversion
- **Footer**: anyone who reaches the bottom of any page and wants to stay connected
- **Resources or blog section**: people who read your content are ideal subscribers
- **Exit-intent pop-up**: appears when the user is about to close the tab (acceptable if it's quick to dismiss)
- **Thank-you page after ticket purchase**: someone who's already bought a ticket is the highest-quality subscriber

## Frequency and content

**Recommended frequency:** monthly for most artists. Bi-monthly if you have a lot of content. More than one email per week is intrusive for a fan list; less than one every two months means people forget you.

**What to send:**
- Upcoming concert or show date (always with a ticket link)
- Latest press mention or review
- Exclusive content for subscribers: making-of, new demo, reflection on your process
- Monthly diary
- Personal and curious content: what's motivating you, what you're reading, what you've just discovered

**What NOT to send:** direct advertising without context, mass emails without any personalisation, content you haven't reviewed.

The newsletter is the channel where you have the most freedom to speak in your own voice. It doesn't need to be perfect: it needs to be yours.
