Most artists waste time on platforms that don't serve their professional goals, while neglecting the ones that could. Getting this right isn't a matter of digital talent — it's understanding who each platform is actually for.

An artist manages two very different audiences: the **public** (who buy tickets, follow the work, share the music) and **industry professionals** (programmers, directors, producers, journalists). Each platform primarily serves one or the other.

### Instagram — for the public and the artistic community

The reference platform for visual artists, musicians and performing arts companies. Useful for building an audience, maintaining a relationship with existing followers, and being visible within the sector's community. Programmers and journalists use it for an informal impression, not for decision-making.

For artists: regular posting (1–3 times per week), visual identity consistent with your brand, stories for day-to-day updates, reels for reach. Avoid mixing professional content with unrelated personal material.

### LinkedIn — for industry professionals

Underused by artists, but this is where programmers, producers, festival directors, agents and managers are. It doesn't work like Instagram (people aren't browsing for entertainment) — it's a professional reference network where your profile appears when someone searches your name or profile type.

For artists: a complete profile with professional history, professional photo, first-person bio (here, unlike an EPK), and occasional updates when there's relevant news (premiere, award, new collaboration). No need to post daily.

### YouTube / Vimeo — for permanent reference material

Not a social network in the classic sense, but this is where reference audiovisual material belongs: the showreel, concert or performance videos, behind-the-scenes content. YouTube is indexed by Google. Vimeo has better quality and is more common in professional film and dance circuits. Both complement the EPK as a place to host embeddable videos.

### TikTok — for musicians and artists with educational or process content

Worth considering for artists who want to grow a younger audience and can generate regular content about the creative process, learning an instrument, or behind-the-scenes of a show. It requires consistency and energy for production. If you don't have the time or inclination, it's not worth it — an abandoned TikTok is worse than not having one.

## What not to do on any professional network

**Mixing the professional account with political opinions or unrelated personal content.** Not because opinions aren't legitimate, but because the professional account is a work tool seen by everyone who hires you or is considering it. A festival programmer who disagrees politically may stop considering you for reasons that have nothing to do with your art.

The solution isn't self-censorship — it's separate accounts. The personal one for opinions and private life, the professional one exclusively for artistic work.

**Posting in bursts with long silences**: three posts in one week then two months of nothing signals inconsistency. One post per week, regularly, is worth far more.

**Trying to maintain all platforms at once**: two well-maintained accounts outperform six abandoned ones. Choose the platforms you can genuinely sustain and let the rest go.

## Realistic posting frequency for artists

You don't need to post every day. You need to post with regularity:

- Instagram: 1–3 times per week is enough to maintain visibility
- LinkedIn: 1–2 updates per month when there's relevant news
- YouTube/Vimeo: when there's new material (no fixed cadence needed)
- TikTok: if you're on it, 3–5 times per week minimum for the algorithm to help

Consistency matters more than volume. Three months of weekly posting outperforms three weeks of daily posts followed by two months of silence.

## Social media and the press kit: the right relationship

Social media doesn't replace the digital press kit. They serve different purposes.

Social media builds audience and relationship over time. The press kit answers specific questions when someone needs to make a decision. A programmer who finds you on Instagram will want the press kit to decide. A journalist who receives your press release will look for the press kit for materials. Social media makes you appear; the press kit gets you hired.
