Some artists walk into a room and, without saying a word, you already have a sense of their music. Some bands — just from a poster — you know whether the concert is for you. Image is not separate from art: it is the first layer of access to it.

Building a style does not mean dressing for social media. It means having clarity about who you are and letting that show.

## Image is communication, not decoration

A festival programmer receives hundreds of applications. Before pressing play, they have already seen your photo. Before reading the bio, they have already read the visual coherence of your project. This is not an unfair rule: it is the reality of how human attention works.

Artists who build consistent images do not do so out of ego. They do it because they understand that their art reaches people through layers, and the first layer is visual.

**Coherence is not uniformity.** You do not have to dress the same way every time or have a static look. It means that your press photo, your Instagram, your website and your concert poster tell the same visual story — even when they change.

## References who got it right

Not to copy them: to understand how the process works.

**David Bowie** built complete visual identities — Ziggy Stardust, Aladdin Sane, the Thin White Duke — that were inseparable from the music of each period. Every transformation was coherent from within.

**Björk** has collaborated since the 1990s with designers, sculptors and visual artists to create complete universes: Alexander McQueen's swan dress, collaboration with Nick Knight, covers shot by Inez & Vinoodh. Her image is a work of art in itself.

**FKA Twigs** works her aesthetic as an extension of her dance and music. Every photo is choreographed like a movement.

**Rosalía** has built an identity that blends flamenco, urban aesthetics and references to Spanish popular culture with impressive visual consistency — from press photos to her live shows.

**Nick Cave** has spent decades with an image that does not change but is absolutely his own: the black, the seriousness, the visual intensity that perfectly anticipates his music.

The question is not "how do I copy them". The question is: **what is your visual universe, and how does it look?**

## How to build your own style

<div class="resource-steps">

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">01</span>

**Start with the music, not the wardrobe.** Listen to your project as if it belonged to someone else. What colour is it? What texture? What environment does it evoke? This is where visual aesthetics begin: inside the music, not in the mirror. Artists who build the strongest images always start with this question.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">02</span>

**Build a moodboard.** Collect visual references that resonate with you — photographers, films, painters, frames, textures, colours. It does not have to be "from your musical style": it can be anything that feels true. Use [Pinterest](https://www.pinterest.com/), [Milanote](https://milanote.com/) or simply a photo folder. The goal is to identify patterns: what attracts you visually and why.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**Identify 3 words.** Reduce your visual universe to three adjectives. Intimate. Dry. Dark. Or: Exuberant. Urban. Feminine. Or: Minimalist. Precise. Cold. These three words become the filter for every visual decision: the clothing, the lighting in photos, the colours of your website, the design of your poster.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**Research professionals in your field.** Look at which stylists, photographers and video directors work with artists similar to you. The right collaboration multiplies the result. A stylist who understands electronic music delivers very different results from one who usually works with street fashion. The professional matters as much as the concept.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">05</span>

**Plan the photo session like a concert.** The photoshoot is where everything materialises. Come with the moodboard, the three words, two or three clothing options to combine. Talk to the photographer days before — not on the day of the session. The more prepared the session is, the more freedom there will be to improvise when needed.
</div>

</div>

## The mistakes almost everyone makes

<div>
<h3>Wearing "just anything" for photos</h3>
<p>The clothes you wear for a photo session are not everyday clothes. They do not need to be expensive or extravagant, but they do need to be a conscious choice. Bring several options, mix them, consult the photographer. Photos last years: the five-minute decision about what you're wearing can haunt you.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="resource-checklist__item">
<span class="resource-checklist__icon" aria-hidden="true">
<svg width="28" height="28" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round"><line x1="18" y1="6" x2="6" y2="18"/><line x1="6" y1="6" x2="18" y2="18"/></svg>
</span>
<div>
<h3>Changing aesthetic every season</h3>
<p>Visual identity needs time to take root. Artists who change it constantly — new logo every year, different aesthetic with each record — make it harder for audiences to build a visual relationship with the project. You can evolve: it is different from erasing and rebuilding.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="resource-checklist__item">
<span class="resource-checklist__icon" aria-hidden="true">
<svg width="28" height="28" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round"><line x1="18" y1="6" x2="6" y2="18"/><line x1="6" y1="6" x2="18" y2="18"/></svg>
</span>
<div>
<h3>Copying the style of someone you admire</h3>
<p>Referencing is legitimate; copying is a strategic error. If your image too closely resembles another artist, everything you communicate is that you are a secondary version of someone else. Influences must be digested and transformed, not reproduced.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="resource-checklist__item">
<span class="resource-checklist__icon" aria-hidden="true">

</span>
<div>
<h3>Leaving image for "when the moment comes"</h3>
<p>The moment does not arrive on its own. The image built in the early stages of a career is what creates audience expectations. Waiting until you are "successful enough" to take care of your image is confusing cause and effect: a professional image is part of what generates success.</p>
</div>

## Where to find inspiration

**Reference photographers** for artist portraits:
- [Anton Corbijn](https://www.antoncorbijn.com/) — 40 years photographing U2, Depeche Mode, Nick Cave
- [Rineke Dijkstra](https://www.moma.org/artists/7050) — minimal portraits of unusual intensity
- [Nan Goldin](https://www.instagram.com/nangoldin/) — intimacy and authenticity as aesthetics

**Instagram accounts** for building your moodboard:
- [@iconicimages](https://www.instagram.com/iconicimages/) — iconic photos of artists from every era
- [@musicphotography](https://www.instagram.com/musicphotography/) — music photography community
- Look up the official photographer of your references: they often publish complete sessions

**Films as visual references** (for composition and colour, not subject matter):
- *Portrait of a Lady on Fire* (Céline Sciamma, 2019) — natural light as aesthetic decision
- *Moonlight* (Barry Jenkins, 2016) — texture and colour as emotion
- *Suspiria* (Luca Guadagnino, 2018) — body, clothing and space as a unit

## Coherence is the brand

Your stage look, your press photo, your Instagram profile and your website do not need to be identical. They need to be unmistakably yours.

When a journalist copies your photo for an article, when a programmer opens your EPK, when someone new lands on your Instagram profile: in each of those moments, your image is working for you. Or it is not.
