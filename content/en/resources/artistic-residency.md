Artistic residencies exist in every discipline and every format: from a week in a rural farmhouse without internet to six months in an international arts centre with a stipend and production costs covered. What they all share is the same premise: time and space to create, away from everyday obligations.

For many artists, a good residency is more transformative than any grant. It doesn't carry the administrative weight of a subsidy, it doesn't demand a finished product on rigid deadlines, and it puts the artist in contact with contexts and people who often open unexpected doors.

But competitive residencies — especially international ones — receive hundreds of applications. Your dossier needs to be specific, relevant and well-argued.

## Types of residencies

Understanding what kind of residency you want (and what for) is the first step to choosing well where to apply.

**By objective:**
- **Creation:** time and space to develop a new project. There's usually no deliverable during the stay, but a subsequent result is expected (premiere, publication, exhibition).
- **Research:** exploring a theme, technique or context. Often linked to residencies at university centres or museums.
- **Production:** the residency finances and facilitates the production of a specific work. Often includes access to a recording studio, workshop, laboratory or stage.
- **In-house residency at a venue or theatre:** a programmer invites you to create a show that their space will co-produce or premiere. One of the most direct ways of entering the professional circuit.

**By format:**
- **Isolated:** the artist works alone in a retreat setting (countryside, mountains, artist houses). Ideal for focus and concentration.
- **Collective:** a group of artists shares space and often process. Generates collaboration and creative cross-pollination.
- **International exchange:** one artist goes to another country while an artist from that country comes to theirs. Programmes such as those from the Institut Ramon Llull or European platforms like Res Artis often work on this logic.

## The residency dossier

Unlike a press kit or a grant dossier, a residency application needs to answer one very specific question: **why do you need this time and this place to do what you want to do?**

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">01</span>

**Specific motivation letter.** Not a generic letter: a letter that demonstrates you know the residency, its context, and why it's relevant to your specific project. Residency selectors read thousands of letters that could apply to any open call. The one that talks about us, about our collection, about the neighbourhood we're in or the artists who've been here — that's the one that stands out.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">02</span>

**Description of the residency project.** What you want to do during the stay, specifically. Not what you want to become or what your work is in general. "During the residency I want to explore the relationship between silence and minimal percussion through daily score-free rehearsals, with the aim of generating material for a 40-minute piece to premiere in 2027" is a residency project. "I want to grow as an artist and explore new horizons" is not.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**Relevant work samples.** Select, don't list everything you've done. Three or four pieces or documents that illustrate where you've come from and where you are now. The samples should be coherent with the project you're proposing: if you're applying for a sound creation residency, don't lead with your track record as a session musician.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**Brief, relevant artistic CV.** Filtered to what matters for that residency. Two pages maximum. Training, key projects, publications, previous residencies if any.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">05</span>

**Work plan.** Some residencies ask for an approximate weekly schedule. Showing that you've thought through how you'll organise your time conveys seriousness. It doesn't need to be exact — it's a plan, not a contract.

## What to expect from a residency

Every residency is different, but some points apply almost universally:

**On production:** Not all residencies guarantee you'll leave with a finished work. Many are designed for research and experimentation, and the outcome may be a documented process, raw materials, or a better-formulated question. Arrive without the expectation of a finished product.

**On community:** If the residency is collective, the other artists present often contribute as much as the formal programme. Reserve time to talk, share processes and collaborate informally.

**On visibility:** Some residencies include a public presentation at the end of the stay. It's an opportunity to show work in progress, not to premiere the definitive piece. Approach it as a process presentation, not an opening night.

**On documentation:** Document the stay for yourself: journals, process notes, photographs. When it's over, you'll have material for future applications and for your own communications. A well-documented residency builds your professional archive.

## If this is your first residency

If you've never done a residency, start with local or regional open calls where international competition is lower. Build documented experience — one well-used residency opens doors to the next. And seek mentorship: artists who have been through similar residencies will help you avoid mistakes and guide you on how to present yourself.
