Artist residencies exist across all disciplines and in all formats: from a week in a rural farmhouse without internet to six months in an international art centre with stipend and funded production. What they all share is the same offer: time and space to create, outside everyday obligations.

For many artists, a good residency is more transformative than any grant. It carries less administrative weight than a scholarship, does not demand a finished product on rigid deadlines, and places the artist in contact with contexts and people that often open unexpected doors.

But competitive residencies — especially international ones — receive hundreds of applications. Your dossier needs to be specific, relevant and well argued.

## Types of residencies

Understanding what type of residency you want (and why) is the first step to choosing well where to apply.

**By objective:**
- **Creation:** time and space to develop a new project. You do not need to deliver anything during the stay, but a subsequent result is usually expected (premiere, publication, exhibition).
- **Research:** exploring a theme, technique or context. Often linked to residencies at university centres or museums.
- **Production:** the residency funds and facilitates the production of a specific work. Often includes access to a recording studio, workshop, laboratory or stage.
- **Residency in a venue or theatre:** a programmer invites you to create a show that their space will co-produce or premiere. One of the most direct forms of integration into the professional circuit.

**By format:**
- **Isolated:** the artist works alone in a retreat environment (countryside, mountains, artist houses). Ideal for deep concentration.
- **Collective:** a group of artists shares space and, often, process. Generates collaboration and creative cross-pollination.
- **International exchange:** one artist goes to another country while an artist from that country comes to theirs. Programmes by networks like [Res Artis](https://resartis.org/), [IETM](https://www.ietm.org/) or national culture institutes often work on this logic.

Many private and international residencies do not actively publish open calls: you find them through other artists, through networks like Res Artis, or through the programme calendars of art centres you follow.

## The residency dossier

Unlike a press kit or grant application, the residency dossier must answer one very specific question: **why do you need this time and this place to do what you want to do?**

**Specific motivation letter.** Not a generic letter: a letter that demonstrates you know the residency, its context, and why it is relevant to your specific project. Residency selectors read thousands of letters that could apply to any open call. The one that speaks about us, our collection, our neighbourhood or the artists who have passed through — that is the one that stays.

**Description of the residency project.** What you want to do during the stay, specifically. Not what you want to become or what your work is in general. "During the residency I want to explore the relationship between silence and minimal percussion through daily score-free rehearsals, with the aim of generating material for a 40-minute piece premiering in 2027" is a residency project. "I want to grow as an artist and explore new horizons" is not.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**Relevant work sample.** Select, do not list everything you have done. Three or four pieces or documents that illustrate where you come from and where you are now. The sample must be coherent with the project you are proposing: if you are applying for a sound creation residency, do not lead with your session musician career.
</div>

<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**Short, relevant artistic CV.** Filtered to what is relevant for that residency. Maximum two pages. Training, notable projects, publications, previous residencies if any.

**Work plan.** Some residencies ask for an approximate weekly schedule. Showing you have thought about how to organise the time conveys seriousness. It does not need to be exact — it is a plan, not a contract.

## What to expect from a residency

Every residency is different, but some points apply almost universally:

**On production:** Not all residencies guarantee you will leave with a finished work. Many are designed for research and experimentation, and the result may be a documented process, raw materials, or a better-formulated question. Arrive without the expectation of a finalised product.

**On community:** In collective residencies, the other artists present often contribute as much as the formal programme. Reserve time for conversation, sharing processes and informal collaboration.

**On visibility:** Some residencies include a public showing at the end of the stay. This is an opportunity to present work in progress, not to premiere the definitive piece. Approach it as a process presentation, not an opening night.

**On documentation:** Document the stay for yourself: journals, process notes, photographs. When you finish, you will have material for future applications and your own communication. A well-documented residency builds your professional archive.

## If this is your first residency

If you have never done a residency, start with local or regional calls with less international competition. Build documented experience — one well-used residency opens doors to the next. And seek mentorship: artists who have been through similar residencies will help you avoid mistakes and guide you on how to present yourself.
