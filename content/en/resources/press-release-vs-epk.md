A cultural journalist receives between twenty and fifty pitches a day from artists wanting coverage. Most are discarded in ten seconds. The fastest way to get discarded: sending the wrong document.

Confusing a press release with an EPK — or sending both at once without explaining what each is for — signals immediately that the artist doesn't understand how professional communication works. A journalist who spots it moves on.

### Press release

Today's news. A short text (300–500 words) that explains **one thing**: a release, an award, a collaboration, an important date. It has a journalistic angle — it answers *who, what, when, where and why now*.

It's sent **when you have a specific piece of news**. When you don't, it's not sent.

### EPK (digital press kit)

Your permanent portfolio. A web page — or web document — with **all your professional information**: bio, high-res photos, videos, contact. It's not a news item: it's who you are.

It's not sent proactively. It's linked or attached as a **complement** to a press release, or shared when someone requests it.

## The most common mistake

An artist releases an album. They write an email to a journalist explaining their background since 2015, attach three files and finish with "sending over some material in case you'd like to cover it."

Problems:
- The journalist can't identify **the journalistic angle** (the news is the album release, but it only appears at the end)
- The email is too long to be a press release and too disorganised to be a useful EPK
- The attachments may be too heavy to open
