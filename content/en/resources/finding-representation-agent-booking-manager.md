Representation is one of the topics that causes the most confusion among emerging artists. The usual question is "do I need an agent?". The honest answer is: it depends on what you want to do, and where you are right now.

First you need to understand who does what — because an agent, a booker and a manager don't do the same thing — and then work out when it makes sense to look for each one.

## Who's who: the three figures

**The booker or booking agent** handles contracting: they're the one who talks to programmers, negotiates fees and organises dates. They charge a percentage of the fees (typically between 10 and 20%). They don't work for you: they work for a roster of artists and put your name forward when you fit what they've been asked for.

**The manager** handles the artist's career in a global sense: strategy, relationships with labels or production companies, image, long-term decisions. They charge a percentage of all the artist's income (10–25% depending on the level). A good manager is a strategic partner. A bad manager is a deduction from your pay packet.

**The actor or dancer's agent** is specific to the audiovisual and performing arts industry: they handle casting calls, company enquiries and contracts. They charge a percentage of each contract (typically 10–15%). You need an agent to access closed castings that aren't published through the usual channels.

## How to get representation: the real process

<div class="resource-steps">

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">01</span>

**Prepare your presentation materials.** A representative needs: a professional bio (100–200 words), press or production photos, audiovisual material (performance video, showreel, link to work), a history of relevant dates or shows, and a press note or project dossier if relevant. Without these, no presentation is possible.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">02</span>

**Identify the agencies and representatives in your circuit.** Not all agencies represent all styles. A jazz booker won't take on a flamenco group. An agent for TV series actors doesn't handle experimental theatre performers. Research who represents artists similar to you and which circuit you want to be part of.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">03</span>

**The first contact: brief, specific, materials ready.** The introductory email to an agency should contain: who you are in two sentences, what material you have available (link), and why you think you fit their roster. Not a one-page cover letter — an email that can be read in 30 seconds with everything needed to make a decision of "interested / not interested".
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">04</span>

**The alternative: come through someone.** Most representatives take on artists through recommendations from other artists or professionals they already know. If you know someone on an agency's roster who can introduce you, the success rate increases enormously. Build your network at festivals, training spaces and events in your discipline.
</div>

<div class="resource-steps__item">
<span class="resource-steps__num">05</span>

**Read the representation contract before signing.** A contract with an agent must specify: duration (avoid open-ended contracts), the applicable percentage and on which income, exclusivity (does it prevent you from working directly with programmers?) and termination conditions. A contract without an end date can tie you to a representative who does nothing for you for years.
</div>

</div>

## Representation is not the solution to every problem

A booker won't do your marketing. A manager won't replace your own network. An actor's agent doesn't guarantee work. Representation multiplies what already works; it doesn't create anything from nothing.

Before looking for representation, make sure your digital identity is in order: website, press kit, press photos, up-to-date profiles. Any potential representative will search your name on Google. What they find is the first impression they'll have of your professionalism.
