The best artistic collaborations don't happen by chance — or rather, the ones that last and produce real work rarely do. They happen because someone made a specific proposal, both parties understood what was being offered, and there was enough clarity about the terms to avoid the friction that kills most collaborations before they produce anything.

## Types of collaboration

Not all collaborations are the same. Three basic categories:

**1. One-off collaboration:** a musician who plays at a concert, a photographer who shoots a premiere, a designer who makes a poster. Simple agreement, clear compensation, no long-term involvement in the project.

**2. Co-creation of a work:** two or more artists create a piece, a show or a visual work together. The question of copyright is central: who is the author of what?

**3. Company or project collaboration:** two or more artists build a long-term project together, with shared economic and decision-making involvement. The risk is higher, and the need for formal agreements is much greater.

## Where to find collaborators

- **Artistic residencies:** the best context for unplanned collaborations. Meeting someone at your level with a complementary discipline during a residency is the best thing that can happen.
- **Courses and masterclasses:** course peers — whether in Catalonia or abroad — are potential collaborators. The intensity of shared learning creates lasting professional bonds.
- **Specific platforms:** [SoundBetter](https://soundbetter.com/) for musicians (producers, session players, singers). [Musicianslist](https://www.musicianslist.com/) for open calls. For performing arts, networks like [Aerowaves](https://aerowaves.org/) and [Circostrada](https://circostrada.org/) facilitate connections between companies.
- **Your existing networks:** an email to an artist you admire proposing a specific collaboration has a much higher success rate than most people think. The key is that the proposal is specific — not "I'd love to collaborate" but "I'm working on X and I think your work in Y would fit because Z."

## The initial proposal

A good collaboration proposal has three parts: why you're approaching this specific person (articulating why them, not just anyone), what you're proposing (the project, format, duration), and what you're bringing to the table. Without all three, the proposal is vague and unlikely to get a response.

What doesn't need to be in the first message: all the financial details, rights, percentages. First confirm there's mutual interest; then discuss the specifics.

## When to formalise

For minor one-off collaborations (one concert, one show), a verbal or email agreement can be sufficient. For co-creation of a work or project collaboration, a written document is essential. Not because you don't trust each other, but because situations change and memories diverge.

The minimum document should cover: who authored what (important for copyright if the work is distributed or published), how any income is divided, how decisions are made when there's disagreement, and what happens if someone wants to leave the project. The article [Basic contracts every musician needs to know](/en/resources/basic-contracts-musicians/) lays out the legal framework.

## When collaboration doesn't work

Collaborations fail for many reasons, but the most common are: misaligned expectations from the start, imbalance of commitment (one person contributing much more than the other), or aesthetic disagreements without a mechanism to resolve them.

The early warning sign: if communications become tense, if you're avoiding conversations that need to happen, if you feel like you're doing everything yourself — stop and talk early. Collaborations left unresolved can end in legal disputes over ownership of the work.

## Collaborating with non-artist professionals

Working with a photographer, a designer or a sound engineer follows the same principles, but there's an important asymmetry: the non-artist professional typically has a more transactional view (services for money) than the artist (who may have fantasies of a "shared project" that the other party doesn't share). Clarify the framework from the start: commission? exchange? co-creation?
